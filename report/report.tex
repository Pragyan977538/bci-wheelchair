\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A brain--computer interface (BCI) that decodes motor imagery from
scalp EEG is a control channel with three severe defects: it is
\emph{wrong} a large fraction of the time, it is \emph{late}, and it is
\emph{low-rate}. On the nine subjects of the BNCI2014\_001 dataset, a
frozen CSP + shrinkage-LDA decoder evaluated strictly across sessions
attains a mean accuracy of $0.443$ against a chance level of $0.25$
($\kappa = 0.258$). A wheelchair driven directly from that channel is
not usable: in simulation it reaches the goal in $11.9\%$ of episodes
and collides in $88.1\%$ --- partly because the simulated operator
steers straight at the goal and does not itself avoid obstacles.

This report studies the control-theoretic remedy: blend the user's
command with the command of an autonomous navigator through a scalar
authority $\alpha \in [0,1]$, and choose $\alpha$ online. Six policies
are compared over a fully paired $32{,}400$-episode experiment. The
proposed law, $\alpha = c\,(1-r)$ with $c$ the calibrated decoder
posterior and $r$ a time-to-collision risk signal, achieves a $99.6\%$
success rate with a $0.4\%$ collision rate while still routing $12\%$ of
authority to the user, and --- the result we consider most important ---
it holds $0.98$--$1.00$ success for \emph{every} subject, including one
whose decoder performs below chance ($\kappa = -0.079$), although that
subject receives only about $3\%$ authority. It therefore
decouples task success from decoder quality, a link that is visible
under fixed blending and under the safety filter (Pearson $r = +0.55$
and $+0.69$ across the nine subjects).

On the control-theory side we derive the transport-delay stability
boundary of the blended loop \emph{exactly} rather than by
approximation. Substituting $s = j\omega$ into the characteristic
equation and imposing $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ causes the delay to cancel
identically, leaving a quadratic in $\omega^2$ whose positive roots are
the crossing frequencies. This yields $\tau_{\max}(\alpha)$ in closed
form, an upper bound $\astar = K_1/(K_1+K_p)$ on the authorities at which
the loop is stable for every delay, and a closed-form criterion,
$b(\astar) \ge 0$, deciding whether that bound is tight. The boundary agrees with an independent
package implementation at $\alpha = 1$ to $2.2\times 10^{-16}$\,s, with
direct delay differential equation (DDE) bisection at six authorities to
$4.6\times10^{-4}\%$, and with
an independent base-MATLAB implementation exactly at $\alpha = 1$ and to
$5\times10^{-10}$\,s over the whole curve.

We report three findings against our own interest. The adaptive policy
is \emph{not} statistically better than simply ceding control to the
autonomy ($\Delta = -0.004$, 95\% CI $[-0.009,\ +0.000]$). Ablation shows that
the \emph{risk} term is what keeps the policy safe: deleting risk costs
$20$ points of success, whereas replacing the measured confidence with a
perfect oracle leaves success unchanged at its ceiling ($0.996$) and
instead doubles the authority returned to the user. And two negative
results are reported as negative: decoder errors show no burst structure
on this dataset, and a minimum-intervention safety filter is safe but
not helpful. The law is accordingly reframed as \emph{risk-gated
authority} rather than confidence-adaptive control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Motivation}

A person with a high-level spinal cord injury or advanced amyotrophic
lateral sclerosis may retain the ability to \emph{imagine} moving a
limb while retaining no ability to move it. Motor imagery produces
measurable, lateralised changes in the $\mu$ (8--13\,Hz) and $\beta$
(13--30\,Hz) rhythms over sensorimotor cortex, and those changes can be
decoded from scalp EEG. This is the physical basis of a motor-imagery
BCI, and it is the only viable control channel for some users.

It is also, by the standards of any other control system, an appalling
one. Table~\ref{tab:decoder} reports what a carefully constructed
decoder actually achieves on nine subjects when it is trained on one
recording session and evaluated on another: a mean accuracy of $0.443$
on a four-class problem. One subject's decoder performs \emph{below
chance}. Decisions arrive roughly every two seconds and are themselves
late by a subject-dependent interval that we measure rather than assume.

Connecting such a channel directly to the wheels of a powered
wheelchair is unsafe, and this is not a matter of opinion: our
simulation puts the collision rate at $88.1\%$. The engineering answer
that the field has converged on is \emph{shared control} --- give the
vehicle its own autonomous navigator and split the control authority
between the two. The question that remains, and the question this
project is about, is how to split it.

\subsection{The research question}

The arbitration law under study is the standard convex blend,

\begin{equation}
u(t) \;=\; \alpha(t)\,\ubci(t) \;+\; \bigl(1-\alpha(t)\bigr)\,\uauto(t),
\qquad \alpha(t) \in [0,1],
\label{eq:arb}
\end{equation}

where $\ubci$ is the command derived from the user's decoded intention
and $\uauto$ is the command produced by an autonomous navigation
controller. Setting $\alpha \equiv 1$ recovers manual driving;
$\alpha \equiv 0$ recovers full autonomy. The research question is:

\begin{quote}
\itshape
Given a command channel that is delayed, low-rate, and wrong 56\% of
the time, how should control authority $\alpha$ be allocated between the
human and the autonomous controller so that the closed loop remains
stable and the navigation task succeeds --- and what is the
delay margin of the resulting loop as a function of $\alpha$?
\end{quote}

It is worth stating plainly what the question is \emph{not}. This is
not a project about decoding EEG better. The decoder is constructed
once, in Stage 1, and is then \textbf{frozen}: no later stage tunes it,
and no reported result depends on improving it. That constraint is
deliberate and is enforced by the repository's own structure --- the
decoder's only outputs to the rest of the system are two files in
\texttt{data/interchange/}. Any effort spent raising decoder accuracy
would be effort taken from the control problem, which is the actual
subject.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An exact transport-delay stability boundary} for the
blended loop, $\tau_{\max}(\alpha)$, obtained by showing that the delay
cancels identically from the magnitude condition, together with a
closed-form criterion in the gains alone for when the delay-independent
authority bound $\astar$ is tight (Section~\ref{sec:math}).

\item \textbf{A four-way verification of that boundary}: against an
independent package implementation, against direct DDE integration
(settle/diverge), against DDE bisection, and against an independent
implementation in base MATLAB with no shared code
(Section~\ref{sec:verification}).

\item \textbf{A fully paired 32{,}400-episode comparison} of six
arbitration policies on real per-subject decoder statistics, showing
that risk-gated authority decouples task success from decoder quality
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\item \textbf{An ablation that contradicts the policy's own name}:
situational risk, not decoder confidence, is what keeps the policy safe;
confidence only sets how much authority is returned
(Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).

\item \textbf{A demonstrated failure mode of convex blending} in the
closed loop with actuator lag: two individually safe commands whose
average collides, in a geometry constructed so that the failure is
guaranteed rather than hoped for (Section~\ref{sec:averaging}).

\item \textbf{Two negative results reported as negative} --- the absence
of burst structure in decoder errors on this dataset, and the
insufficiency of minimum-intervention safety filtering
(Section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reproducibility contract}

Every number in this report is reproducible. The root seed is fixed
(\texttt{meta.seed: 20260901}); every random draw in the system descends
from it; and reruns are bit-identical, not merely statistically similar.
All parameters live in \texttt{config/}, and the repository holds itself

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Brain-actuated wheelchairs and shared control.} Driving a
wheelchair from a motor-imagery BCI has been pursued for nearly two
decades, and the consistent finding is that raw decoder output is not
usable on its own. Vanacker et al.~\cite{vanacker2007context} introduced
context-based filtering for assisted brain-actuated driving, in which
the environment constrains what the decoded intent is allowed to mean;
Carlson and Mill\'an~\cite{carlson2013wheelchairs} developed that into a
full robotic architecture and showed asynchronous motor-imagery control
outperforming synchronous P300 alternatives. This report inherits their
premise --- assistance is mandatory, not optional --- and differs in what
it measures: rather than demonstrating a working system, it isolates
\emph{which term} of the arbitration law is responsible for the
assistance, and reports that the answer is not the one the policy is
named after.

\textbf{Arbitration as policy blending.} The law
$u = \alpha\,\ubci + (1-\alpha)\,\uauto$ used here is an instance of the
policy-blending formalism of Dragan and
Srinivasa~\cite{dragan2013blending}, who separate assistance into
\emph{prediction} of user intent and \emph{arbitration} between that
prediction and the user's input, and observe that most shared-control
schemes differ only in how arbitration is chosen. Abbink et
al.~\cite{abbink2018topology} give a broader topology and a set of design
axioms for the field. The present work is a study of the arbitration
function alone, holding prediction fixed at whatever the decoder
supplies.

\textbf{Motor-imagery decoding.} The neurophysiological basis is
event-related desynchronisation over sensorimotor cortex during imagined
movement~\cite{pfurtscheller2001mi}. The decoding pipeline used here is
deliberately conventional so that the control results are not confounded
by decoder novelty: common spatial patterns~\cite{ramoser2000csp} for
spatial filtering, linear discriminant analysis regularised by
shrinkage~\cite{blankertz2011shrinkage}, and Platt
scaling~\cite{platt1999sigmoid} to turn discriminant scores into
calibrated posteriors --- the posteriors that the arbitration law then
consumes as ``confidence''. Data are the four-class Graz set
distributed as BCI Competition~IV dataset~2a~\cite{brunner2008graz},
accessed through MOABB~\cite{jayaram2018moabb} and
MNE-Python~\cite{gramfort2013mne} so that the preprocessing is the
community-standard one rather than a bespoke variant.

\textbf{Autonomy and safety.} The autonomous partner is a dynamic-window
planner~\cite{fox1997dwa}, chosen because it is derived from the
vehicle's own velocity and acceleration limits, which is what makes the
infeasibility result of Section~\ref{sec:unrecoverable} interpretable: when all
candidates in the dynamic window collide, that is a statement about the
chair's dynamics rather than about a planner's parameters. The
minimum-intervention filter studied here is a time-to-collision
heuristic; the modern formulation of the same idea is the control barrier
function~\cite{ames2019cbf}, which would replace the heuristic with a
forward-invariance guarantee and is a natural next step.

\textbf{Delay.} The stability analysis follows standard frequency-domain
treatment of retarded systems~\cite{gu2003delay}: substituting
$s = j\omega$ into the characteristic quasi-polynomial and solving for
the crossing frequency. What is specific here is that the delay cancels
identically, reducing an infinite-dimensional problem to a quadratic, and
that the resulting delay-independent bound admits a closed-form
tightness criterion (Section~\ref{sec:math}).

\section{System Architecture}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Signal flow}

Figure~\ref{fig:block} shows the closed loop. Reading it from the left:
the simulated operator observes the plant state \emph{as it was
$\tau_h$ seconds ago}, forms an intent, and that intent passes through
a command channel that samples it at the decoder's decision rate,
delays it by a measured latency, and corrupts it while the decoder is
in its degraded state. In parallel, the autonomous layer plans from the
\emph{true} current state. The arbitration block combines the two using
$\alpha$, which is itself computed from the decoder's calibrated
confidence and from a situational risk signal derived from the
time-to-collision of the command the user is asking for.

The ordering of operations within one simulation step is not arbitrary
and is worth stating explicitly, because several plausible orderings
silently destroy the phenomenon under study:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The operator reads the state as it was $\tau_h$ ago and forms an intent.
\item The channel samples that intent at the decision rate, delays it by
      a sampled latency, corrupts it if degraded, and reports a confidence.
\item The intent is converted to a plant command using the
      \emph{operator's delayed view} --- a human acts on the world as they
      last saw it, not on the world as it is.
\item The autonomous layer plans from the \emph{true} current state,
      replanning at $10$\,Hz and holding between plans.
\item The risk signal is evaluated on $\ubci$, not on the blend.
\item Arbitration produces $u$; the plant advances by $dt = 0.02$\,s.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Configuration and determinism}

All parameters --- filter bands, gains, thresholds, sweep ranges,
ablation definitions --- live in \texttt{config/default.yaml} and
\texttt{config/environments.yaml}. The loader supports dotted access and
non-mutating overrides, and \emph{raises} on an override that names a
key which does not exist. That last detail is not fussiness: four of the
six ablations were initially written against config keys that did not
exist, so they would have applied nothing and been faithfully reported
as ``no effect''. The test
\texttt{test\_every\_declared\_ablation\_targets\_a\_real\_key} caught
all four.

Determinism is enforced at the episode level. Each episode's generator
is seeded from the tuple $(\text{root seed}, \text{subject},
\text{environment}, \text{seed index}, \text{burst setting})$. The
\emph{variant is deliberately excluded} from that key, so every
arbitration policy meets a bit-identical realisation of decoder errors,
latencies and confusions. This is what makes the paired statistics of
Section~\ref{sec:results} legitimate rather than decorative, and it is
asserted by
\texttt{test\_variants\_are\_paired\_on\_identical\_noise}.

\section{The BCI Data Pipeline}
\label{sec:bci}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and preprocessing}

We use BNCI2014\_001 (BCI Competition IV dataset 2a) through MOABB:
nine subjects, four motor-imagery classes (left hand, right hand, feet,
tongue), two sessions recorded on separate days, $288$ trials per
session, 22 EEG channels at $250$\,Hz. Training uses session
\texttt{0train}; evaluation uses session \texttt{1test}. No trial
crosses between them, so the evaluation is strictly cross-session ---
the harder and more honest split, since a real user does not recalibrate
mid-day.

Preprocessing is a fourth-order Butterworth bandpass at $8$--$30$\,Hz
(covering $\mu$ and $\beta$), EOG channels dropped, and epochs taken
from $0.5$ to $2.5$\,s relative to \emph{cue onset}. No baseline
correction is applied, because the feature is band power and baseline
subtraction would distort it.

\begin{remark}[A silent labelling trap]
The dataset advertises the event mapping
\[
\{\,\text{left\_hand}{:}1,\;\text{right\_hand}{:}2,\;
   \text{feet}{:}3,\;\text{tongue}{:}4\,\},
\]
whereas MNE's \texttt{events\_from\_annotations} returns events in
\emph{alphabetical} order, giving
\[
\{\,\text{feet}{:}1,\;\text{left\_hand}{:}2,\;
   \text{right\_hand}{:}3,\;\text{tongue}{:}4\,\}.
\]
Using the advertised map permutes every label in
the dataset, and the resulting accuracy remains entirely plausible ---
it simply measures nothing. Labels are therefore resolved by
\emph{name} only, pinned by
\texttt{tests/test\_decoder\_labels.py}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Decoder and calibration}

Features are six Common Spatial Pattern components with log-variance
pooling; the classifier is Linear Discriminant Analysis with
Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage. This is a deliberately conventional pipeline:
the project needs a decoder that is representative, not one that is
optimal.

Raw LDA scores are not probabilities, and the arbitration law consumes
$\alpha = c$ directly in one variant, so an over-confident decoder does
not merely mispredict --- it \emph{hands authority to a wrong command}.
We therefore apply Platt scaling fitted on a stratified $25\%$ holdout
carved from the training session only. Calibration quality is measured
by expected calibration error (ECE) over ten bins.

\begin{remark}[$\kappa$ carries no information beyond accuracy here]
BNCI2014\_001 is exactly balanced, 72 trials per class per session.
Chance agreement is then
$p_e = \sum_i (\hat n_i/N)(n_i/N) = \tfrac14 \sum_i \hat n_i/N = 0.25$
\emph{regardless of how the predictions are distributed}, so
$\kappa = (\text{accuracy} - 0.25)/0.75$ identically. Reporting both as
independent evidence would overstate the case. This identity is pinned
by a unit test.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The mean is low, and it is not leakage]
Mean $\kappa = 0.258$ sits below the $\approx 0.4$ often quoted for this
dataset. We checked rather than assumed. Leakage would \emph{inflate} accuracy, so it
cannot explain a low mean; what needs explaining is the gap between
within-session cross-validation and cross-session evaluation (subject 3:
$0.784$ in cross-validation, $0.691$ on the held-out day). The fitting,
calibration and evaluation sets are disjoint by construction, which
\texttt{test\_decoder\_leakage.py} pins. Subjects 4 and 9 score slightly
\emph{higher} on the held-out session than in cross-validation ($0.389$
vs.\ $0.323$; $0.337$ vs.\ $0.330$), which is consistent with this but
too small to prove anything on its own. Subject 3 reaching $0.784$ in
cross-validation shows the pipeline is capable. The gap is genuine
cross-session non-stationarity --- which is exactly the phenomenon this
project exists to control for. Published higher figures generally use
filter-bank CSP and/or within-session validation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Measuring decision latency}

A cued paradigm has no natural decision latency: the subject is told
when to imagine. Rather than invent a constant, we \emph{measure} one.
The frozen decoder's own $2$\,s window is slid across the imagery period
in $0.1$\,s steps from $0$ to $4$\,s post-cue, and latency is defined as
the end of the first window whose calibrated posterior for the
\texttt{argmax} class crosses $0.5$.

Trials that never cross are \textbf{censored at the $4$\,s horizon and
flagged}, never dropped. Dropping them would bias the distribution
toward exactly the confident trials, understating latency. Censoring is
strongly subject-dependent (from $0.02$ for subject 3 to $1.00$ for
subject 4) and is itself a usable signal: a subject whose decoder never
becomes confident should not be handed authority.

Latency is later sampled by bootstrap rather than fitted to a
parametric family, because several subjects show a bimodal
distribution --- an early mode where the decoder becomes confident, and
a spike at the censoring horizon where it never does.

\subsection{The command-error model, and a negative result}
\label{sec:markov}

The project brief specified, correctly as a general principle, that
decoder errors should \emph{not} be modelled as i.i.d.\
$\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)$ at the marginal error rate $\pi$, but as a
two-state Markov
process over $\{\text{reliable}, \text{degraded}\}$, so that errors
arrive in bursts as they do in practice. We fitted that chain per
subject by maximum likelihood on transition counts with Laplace
smoothing, respecting run boundaries (a session is six runs separated by
rest breaks; the last trial of one run and the first of the next are
minutes apart and are not a transition).

\label{tab:markov}

The premise does not hold on this dataset, as
Table~\ref{tab:markov} shows. Before reporting a null we
established that the test has power, using synthetic sequences of
identical length and run structure: at true $p(d{\to}d) = 0.85$ the test
fires at $p < 0.0001$ ($\mathrm{LR} = 108.4$), and at $0.70$ at
$p<0.0001$ ($\mathrm{LR}=66.8$); it correctly declines to fire when
clustering is marginal. The null on real data is therefore a
measurement, not blindness.

There is a structural explanation. Error rate depends overwhelmingly on
\emph{which class was cued} --- subject 1's per-class error rates are
$(0.556, 0.306, 0.847, 0.069)$, a spread of $0.778$, because the
decoder is biased toward \texttt{tongue}. BNCI2014\_001 randomises cue
order by design, so correctness inherits that randomisation and any slow
drift in the subject's state is swamped by the class effect. The absence
of bursts is a property of \emph{this cued, balanced, randomised
laboratory paradigm}, not evidence that BCI errors are generally
memoryless; a self-paced continuous-control BCI would very plausibly
show clustering.

Rather than fake the phenomenon or delete the machinery, burstiness was
promoted from an assumption to a \textbf{swept independent variable}.
The chain can be re-parameterised to impose a target mean burst length
$B$ while holding each subject's measured error rate $\pi$ fixed:

\begin{equation}
p_{d\to r} = \frac{1}{B},
\qquad
p_{r \to d} = \frac{\pi\, p_{d \to r}}{1-\pi},
\qquad
p_{d\to d} = 1 - p_{d\to r}.
\label{eq:burst}
\end{equation}

At $B = 1/(1-\pi)$ the transitions become memoryless with error
probability $\pi$, so the i.i.d.\ transition condition is a \emph{point
on the sweep} rather than a separate condition. That point is
\textbf{subject-specific}: $B_{\mathrm{iid}} = 1/(1-\pi_i)$ ranges from
$1.45$ (subject 3) to $5.24$ (subject 5). The sweep is therefore
parameterised as a \emph{multiplier} of each subject's own anchor,
$\{1.0, 1.5, 2.5, 4.0\} \times B_{\mathrm{iid},i}$, where $1.0$ is
exactly i.i.d.\ for every subject.

An earlier version used a single global $B \in \{1.8, 3.0, 5.0, 8.0\}$.
That was a different experimental condition for each subject --- for
subject 5 it ran from $0.34\times$ to $1.53\times$ that subject's i.i.d.\
point, i.e.\ mostly \emph{anti}-clustered --- and its two lowest values
were infeasible: \eqref{eq:burst} requires $B \geq \pi/(1-\pi)$, which is
$4.24$ for subject 5, and below it $p_{r \to d}$ exceeds 1, is clipped,
and the chain's error rate silently departs from the measured value by up
to $0.17$. The construction now refuses such values rather than clipping
them. Section~\ref{sec:burstresults} reports what the corrected sweep can
and cannot answer.

\subsection{From four discrete classes to a continuous command}
\label{sec:modality}

This is the most consequential modelling decision in the project and it
deserves to be stated in the open rather than buried.

A real four-class motor-imagery BCI emits one of four discrete symbols
every couple of seconds. The loop analysed in Section~\ref{sec:math},
by contrast, is a continuous linear loop --- it must be, for a
characteristic equation to exist at all. We resolve this by making
$\ubci$ the \emph{continuous} delayed-PD command, and using the real
decoder to supply three things that are genuinely measured:

\begin{itemize}
\item the calibrated posterior $c(t)$ that drives $\alpha$;
\item the \emph{timing} of errors, through the fitted Markov chain, and
      the latency with which commands arrive;
\item the \emph{geometry} of errors --- while degraded, the command is
      rotated toward the class that the subject's own confusion matrix
      says they are confused with, drawn with the diagonal removed and
      rows renormalised.
\end{itemize}

The consequence is favourable for the analysis and unfavourable for
realism, and both halves must be said. Favourably: the command value
is continuous, as the linear analysis of Section~\ref{sec:math}
requires. This does \emph{not} make the simulated loop identical to the
analysed one: Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth} shows that its timing and its
structure differ, and that the analytic boundary does not transfer.
Unfavourably: the simulated channel is smoother than a
real four-class BCI, whose output is a step function on a four-point
alphabet. The error \emph{rate}, \emph{timing} and \emph{confusion
structure} are measured; the error \emph{waveform} is not. We return to
this in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Plant Models}
\label{sec:plants}
% =====================================================================

Two plants share one arbitration layer. Both expose the same interface
(\texttt{reset}, \texttt{step}, \texttt{observation},
\texttt{command\_from\_intent}), so a controller written against one
drives the other unchanged.

\subsection{Plant A: the double integrator}

Per axis, with velocity damping $d$,

\begin{equation}
\dot p = v, \qquad \dot v = u - d\,v,
\label{eq:di}
\end{equation}

state $[p_x, p_y, v_x, v_y]$, input $u \in \mathbb{R}^2$ saturated at
$6$\,m/s$^2$. This is the analytically tractable plant, and it exists so
that Section~\ref{sec:math} has a loop it can solve exactly.

The discrete update is the \emph{exact} zero-order-hold solution,
obtained from the augmented matrix exponential

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} A_d & B_d \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}
= \exp\!\left(
\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} h \right),
\qquad
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -d\end{bmatrix},
\quad
B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:zoh}
\end{equation}

Using the exact solution rather than a numerical integrator has three
benefits: the $d \to 0$ limit needs no special case, there is no
integration error at any step size, and the update is testable in ways
an approximate one is not. It is verified against a $200{,}000$-step
explicit integration for $d \in \{0, 0.3, 1, 2.5\}$ at three step sizes;
by the identity that two half-steps equal one whole step to $10^{-14}$
(true only of an exact update); and by the closed-form reduction at
$d = 0$ to $c_1 = h$, $c_2 = h^2/2$.

\subsection{Plant B: the unicycle wheelchair}

The headline demonstration plant is a differential-drive chair with
first-order motor lag:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot x &= v\cos\theta, &\qquad \dot v &= (v_{\mathrm{cmd}} - v)/\tau_v, \\
\dot y &= v\sin\theta, &\qquad \dot\omega &= (\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}} - \omega)/\tau_\omega, \\
\dot\theta &= \omega, & &
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:uni}
\end{equation}

state $[x, y, \theta, v, \omega]$, input $[v_{\mathrm{cmd}},
\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}}]$, with $\tau_v = 0.30$\,s,
$\tau_\omega = 0.20$\,s, $v \in [-0.4, 1.2]$\,m/s,
$|\omega| \le 1.5$\,rad/s, and rate saturation at $1.0$\,m/s$^2$ and
$3.0$\,rad/s$^2$. Collision radius is $0.35$\,m.

\textbf{The lag is the point.} A wheelchair does not adopt a commanded
velocity instantly, so a command issued now takes effect over $\tau_v$
and $\tau_\omega$ seconds. This actuator lag adds phase lag on top of the
decoder latency and the operator's own sensorimotor delay. It is plant
dynamics, not transport delay: in the heading-loop model of
Section~\ref{sec:headingloop} it appears as the $\tau_\omega\ddot\theta$
term, while the transport delays make up $\tau$. A pure kinematic unicycle
would delete exactly the dynamics being studied.

Nonholonomy is what makes this plant interesting: the chair cannot
translate sideways, so a lateral correction must be achieved by turning,
which takes time it may not have. Integration is fixed-step RK4, checked
against the three cases that do have closed forms --- straight-line
motion ($10^{-9}$ relative), constant-curvature arcs
$x = x_0 + (v/\omega)(\sin(\theta_0 + \omega t) - \sin\theta_0)$
($10^{-6}$ absolute), and the first-order lag step response
$v(t) = v_{\mathrm{cmd}}(1 - e^{-t/\tau_v})$ ($10^{-6}$, every step).
Lateral displacement per step is below $10^{-12}$, so the nonholonomic
constraint is verified numerically rather than merely asserted.

\subsection{Intent to command, and the origin of the averaging failure}
\label{sec:intent}

Both the operator and the autonomy produce a world-frame intent vector;
the plant converts it to its own input space:

\begin{equation}
\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}} = k_\theta\,\mathrm{wrap}(\psi - \theta),
\qquad
v_{\mathrm{cmd}} = \lVert \text{intent}\rVert \cdot
\bigl(\max(\cos(\psi - \theta),\,0)\bigr)^{\,n},
\label{eq:intent}
\end{equation}

with $\psi = \operatorname{atan2}(\text{intent}_y, \text{intent}_x)$,
$k_\theta = 2.0$ and $n = 2$. The exponentiated cosine is a
\emph{speed gate}: a chair pointed $90^\circ$ away from where the
operator wants to go does not drift sideways, it stops and turns. This
is the nonholonomic constraint biting at the command interface.

Equation~\eqref{eq:intent} is also where the averaging failure is born.
Two intents symmetric about an obstacle produce yaw commands of opposite
sign and near-equal magnitude, so their convex blend has
$\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}} \approx 0$ while both retain \emph{undiminished}
forward speed. Section~\ref{sec:averaging} demonstrates the consequence.

Note that arbitration is performed in the plant's input space,
\emph{not} in a normalised intent space. Blending direction-only intents
would renormalise the result and hide this failure behind the
normalisation --- the blend would still point somewhere sensible. It is
the fact that speed survives while yaw cancels that makes the failure
physical.

\subsection{Environments and geometry queries}

Three occupancy-grid environments at $0.05$\,m resolution:
\textbf{corridor} (a $12 \times 2$\,m straight corridor, where operator
and autonomy largely agree, isolating noise and delay from
disagreement); \textbf{doorway} (a central pillar with a passable gap on
each side); and \textbf{cluttered} (an open $12\times10$\,m room with six
scattered obstacles requiring several direction changes, so
time-to-collision varies continuously and the risk term is exercised
across its whole range rather than saturating).

The doorway is constructed so that the averaging failure is
\emph{geometrically guaranteed rather than hoped for}: the pillar spans
$[4.6, 5.4]\times[3.2, 4.8]$ with safe gaps centred at $(5.0, 2.4)$ and
$(5.0, 5.6)$, symmetric about the start--goal line at $y = 4.0$. The two
locally optimal actions are therefore mirror images, and their convex
blend points exactly at the obstacle.

Two implementation decisions keep a $32{,}400$-episode budget tractable.
Clearance is read from a \textbf{Euclidean distance transform} computed
once per environment, making collision tests and DWA's clearance term
$O(1)$ lookups. Ray casting is \textbf{vectorised over samples and beams
together} --- one gather and one \texttt{argmax} instead of roughly $800$
Python iterations per observation. Measured cost is $0.281$\,ms per
step, $0.84$\,s per $60$\,s episode. Range readings are verified against
analytic geometry: from the corridor mid-line a beam at angle $a$ should
hit at $1/\sin a$, and measured ranges match at $90^\circ$, $60^\circ$
and $30^\circ$ to within $0.08$\,m, one and a half cells.

% =====================================================================
\section{The Operator Model}
\label{sec:operator}
% =====================================================================

The human is modelled as a delayed proportional--derivative controller
that knows the goal:

\begin{equation}
\text{intent}(t) = K_p\bigl(g - p(t-\tau_h)\bigr) - K_d\,v(t-\tau_h),
\qquad K_p = 4.0,\; K_d = 2.0,\; \tau_h = 0.20\,\mathrm{s}.
\label{eq:operator}
\end{equation}

The intent is emitted as a world-frame vector, and the plant converts it
through \eqref{eq:intent} using \emph{the same delayed state} --- a
human acts on the world as they last saw it, not on the world as it is.

The operator's \emph{intent} is never corrupted; all corruption lives in
the channel. That separation is deliberate, but it has a consequence that must be
stated: the operator model has \emph{no obstacle term}. Its intent always
points at the goal, so in the doorway (pillar on the start--goal line)
and in the cluttered room a perfectly decoded command still drives into
an obstacle. In the main grid, \vmanual{} fails in all $110$ doorway and
cluttered episodes in which the decoder made no error at all
(Figure~\ref{fig:paths} shows one of them). Degradation observed
downstream is therefore attributable to transmission \emph{and} to an
obstacle-blind operator, not to transmission alone
(Section~\ref{sec:operatorlimits}).

\subsection{The transport delay, and a trap worth documenting}

Transport delay is implemented as a ring buffer of exactly $n =
\tau_h/dt$ slots with strict \textbf{read-before-write}. At step $k$ the
slot about to be overwritten, $k \bmod n$, still holds the value written
at step $k - n$; read it first, then overwrite:

\begin{verbatim}
    idx = k % n
    out = buf[idx]        # written at step k - n
    buf[idx] = value      # ... now becomes step k
    k += 1
    return out
\end{verbatim}

There are three ways to get this wrong and all three are silent:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Implementation & Actual delay \\
\midrule
ring of $n+1$ slots, read-before-write & $n+1$ steps \\
\texttt{deque(maxlen=n)}, append-then-read & $n-1$ steps \\
unfilled deque, append-then-read & \textbf{0 steps} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last is the dangerous one: no crash, no visibly wrong trajectory,
and the entire phenomenon under study quietly deleted. The regression
test drives a ramp through the buffer and asserts
$\text{delayed}[k] = \max(k-n, 0)$ \emph{exactly} for
$n \in \{1,2,5,10,40\}$; a companion test asserts the returned value is
never the value just pushed. Construction raises if $\tau_h$ is not a
whole number of steps, rather than silently rounding the loop delay.

\begin{remark}
This trap is not hypothetical. A diagnostic accessor,
\texttt{perceived\_state()}, originally re-read the ring slot the intent
had just used --- by which time it held the \emph{current} sample. It
returned the undelayed state: precisely the bug the module exists to
prevent, reintroduced in a debugging convenience. It now caches the
value actually returned.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The command channel}

Between the operator and the arbitration layer sits the channel that
makes this a BCI problem rather than a joystick problem. It performs, in
order: zero-order hold at the decoder's decision rate
($2.0$\,s nominal cue-to-cue spacing); a latency queue in which the
newest arrival wins; and corruption while the Markov chain is in its
degraded state, implemented as a rotation of the intent toward the
archetype direction of the class the subject's real confusion matrix
says they are confused with. Magnitude is preserved under rotation
(except for the \texttt{stop} archetype, which has no direction and
zeroes the command), so corruption changes \emph{where} the user is
asking to go, not \emph{how hard} they are asking.

Confidence is held between decisions by zero-order hold, with an
optional age decay
$c(t) = c_0 + (p_{\mathrm{cal}} - c_0)e^{-\mathrm{age}/\tau_c}$
implemented but switched \emph{off} by default and evaluated only as an
ablation --- so that the extra parameter has to earn its place rather
than being assumed.

\subsection{The autonomous layer: LQR for the double integrator}
\label{sec:lqr}

For Plant A the autonomous law is the exact infinite-horizon LQR
minimising

\begin{equation}
J = \int_0^\infty \Bigl( q_p\,p^2 + q_v\,v^2 + r\,u^2 \Bigr)\,dt,
\qquad q_p = 10,\; q_v = 1,\; r = 1,
\end{equation}

per axis. With $A = \begin{bsmallmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & -d\end{bsmallmatrix}$,
$B = \begin{bsmallmatrix}0\\1\end{bsmallmatrix}$ and
$Q = \mathrm{diag}(q_p, q_v)$, the algebraic Riccati equation
$A^{\!\top} P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^{\!\top}P + Q = 0$ with
$P = \begin{bsmallmatrix}p_{11} & p_{12}\\ p_{12} & p_{22}\end{bsmallmatrix}$
gives three scalar equations:

\begin{align}
\text{(1,1)}: &\quad -p_{12}^2/r + q_p = 0, \label{eq:care1}\\
\text{(1,2)}: &\quad p_{11} - d\,p_{12} - p_{12}p_{22}/r = 0, \label{eq:care2}\\
\text{(2,2)}: &\quad 2p_{12} - 2d\,p_{22} - p_{22}^2/r + q_v = 0. \label{eq:care3}
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:care1} gives $p_{12} = \sqrt{r q_p}$ directly, and
\eqref{eq:care3} is a quadratic in $p_{22}$ with a unique positive root.
With $K = R^{-1}B^{\!\top}P = [K_1, K_2]$,

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
K_1 = \sqrt{\frac{q_p}{r}},
\qquad
K_2 = -d + \sqrt{d^2 + \frac{q_v + 2\sqrt{r q_p}}{r}}\;}
\label{eq:lqrgains}
\end{equation}

which for $d = 1$ evaluates to
$K_1 = \sqrt{10} = 3.1622776601683795$ and
$K_2 = 1.885230548905366$. These reproduce a prior standalone study's
independently verified values to twelve digits, which checks the port
rather than merely the arithmetic. The closed loop is
$\ddot p + (d + K_2)\dot p + K_1 p = 0$.

\begin{remark}[Why the LQR carries no obstacle term]
Section~\ref{sec:math} derives the stability boundary for this loop. A
repulsive obstacle term would make it nonlinear and invalidate the
derivation, so the term exists in the code but is disabled unless an
occupancy grid is supplied, and the stability experiments supply none.
\texttt{test\_lqr\_stays\_linear\_without\_a\_grid} asserts
superposition holds.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The autonomous layer: DWA for the unicycle}
\label{sec:dwa}

Plant B is nonholonomic and lives among obstacles, so its autonomous
layer is the Dynamic Window Approach. Every planning period
($0.10$\,s, i.e.\ $10$\,Hz) we:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Construct the \emph{dynamic window} --- the set of
      $(v, \omega)$ reachable within the reachability horizon
      $\Delta t_w = 0.25$\,s given the acceleration limits, sampled on a
      $7 \times 21 = 147$ point grid.
\item Predict each candidate forward for $1.5$\,s. Under constant
      $(v,\omega)$ the unicycle traces exactly the circular arc verified
      in Section~\ref{sec:plants}, so all $147$ candidates are evaluated
      as one vectorised closed-form expression rather than $11{,}025$
      integration steps.
\item Discard candidates whose minimum clearance falls below
      $\rho + \delta$, with $\rho = 0.35$\,m the collision radius and
      $\delta = 0.05$\,m a safety margin.
\item Score the survivors on
      \[
        J = w_h\,\Phi_{\text{progress}}
          + w_c\,\frac{\min(\text{clearance},\, c_{\max})}{c_{\max}}
          + w_v\,v ,
      \]
      with $(w_h, w_c, w_v) = (0.8, 0.4, 0.2)$ and $c_{\max} = 2$\,m.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Why the window must not be sized by the simulation step]
The dynamic window is the set of velocities reachable within one
\emph{planning} interval, which is not the simulation step. Sizing it by
$dt = 0.02$\,s yields a window of $\pm 0.02$\,m/s: every candidate is
then near-identical and the planner is paralysed. This was observed
directly --- DWA returned $[0.02, -0.06]$, creeping forward at $2$\,cm/s
because it could never propose anything faster. Separating
$\Delta t_w = 0.25$\,s (reachability) from $0.10$\,s (replan rate) from
$0.02$\,s (integration) gives a usable window,
$v \in [-0.25, 0.25]$, $\omega \in [-0.75, 0.75]$, with $131$ of $147$
candidates feasible.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The clearance cap is load-bearing]
Without saturating the clearance term, DWA maximises distance from every
obstacle and refuses to enter a doorway at all, because hugging open
space always outscores passing through a gap.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection*{The navigation field: eliminating local minima}

The heading term $\Phi_{\text{progress}}$ deserves particular attention,
because the obvious choice for it is wrong in a way that a unit test
cannot catch.

Scoring ``how well does this trajectory end up pointing at the goal''
using the straight-line direction to the goal makes DWA a greedy local
optimiser on a potential field with local minima. In the doorway
environment the pillar sits symmetrically astride the start--goal line,
so left and right score \emph{identically} and the planner stalls. This
was not caught until the full experiment grid ran and reported
\vauto{} --- the baseline that was supposed to be trivially safe ---
at \textbf{0\% success}.

The fix is to score progress down a \emph{geodesic navigation field}
$\Phi$, computed once per environment by Dijkstra's algorithm on the
8-connected graph of free cells with the goal as source. $\Phi(c)$ is
the true geodesic distance to the goal through free space, and it has
the property that \emph{every} free cell has a strictly downhill
neighbour --- so it has no local minima by construction. The candidate
score becomes the reduction in $\Phi$ along the predicted arc.

The property is now asserted directly by
\texttt{test\_navigation\_field\_has\_no\_local\_minimum}, which samples
free cells and requires each to have a strictly lower neighbour, so a
future change to the geometry or the inflation radius cannot silently
reintroduce the stall.

\subsection{The situational risk signal}
\label{sec:risk}

The adaptive arbitrator needs a scalar $r \in [0,1]$ describing how
dangerous the present situation is, \emph{independently} of how much the
decoder is trusted. The two signals answer different questions, and this
distinction is the conceptual core of the project:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
confidence $c$ & \emph{``is this command what the user meant?''} \\
risk $r$       & \emph{``does it matter if it isn't?''} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A wrong command in the middle of an empty corridor is harmless; the same
command half a metre from a door frame is a collision. Risk is derived
from the time-to-collision $\ttc$ of the \emph{currently commanded}
trajectory through a saturating ramp,

\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{raw}} =
\begin{cases}
0, & \ttc \ge H,\\[2pt]
\dfrac{H - \ttc}{H - F}, & F < \ttc < H,\\[6pt]
1, & \ttc \le F,
\end{cases}
\qquad H = 3.0\,\mathrm{s},\; F = 0.5\,\mathrm{s},
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}

followed by a first-order lag with $\tau_r = 0.30$\,s:
$r_{k+1} = r_k + \bigl(1 - e^{-dt/\tau_r}\bigr)(r_{\mathrm{raw}} - r_k)$.

The lag is not cosmetic. Because $r$ enters $\alpha$, and $\alpha$
multiplies a feedback path, a risk signal that jumps makes the closed
loop time-varying \emph{at the same timescale as the dynamics it
regulates}, and the frozen-$\alpha$ stability boundary of
Section~\ref{sec:math} stops being a meaningful reference. The lag removes
such jumps. It does not, however, make $r$ slow compared with the loop:
$\tau_r = 0.30$\,s is no longer than the loop's own dominant time
constants (about $0.3$--$0.85$\,s across the authority range for the
delay-free linear models of Section~\ref{sec:math}) and much shorter
than their oscillation periods (about $2.5$\,s or longer; at low
authority the heading-loop model does not oscillate at all). The lag
therefore prevents chatter but does not by itself justify treating
$\alpha$ as frozen; Section~\ref{sec:proofscope} returns to this.

\subsubsection*{The adaptive law}

The raw target is the product $c\,(1-r)$: authority is granted only when
the command is \emph{both} trusted (high $c$) \emph{and} safe to obey
(low $r$), and it is withdrawn when \emph{either} condition fails. When
the gate below is open the target is $c\,(1-r)$; when it is closed the
target is $\alpha_{\min} = 0$. Three
pieces of conditioning sit on top of the product:

\begin{align}
\text{Schmitt gate:}\quad
&\text{open if } c(1-r) > 0.60;\quad \text{close if } c(1-r) < 0.40;
\quad \text{hold in between}, \\
\text{Low-pass:}\quad
&\alpha^{-} \leftarrow \alpha + \bigl(1 - e^{-dt/\tau_s}\bigr)
   (\alpha_{\mathrm{target}} - \alpha),
   \qquad \tau_s = 0.25\,\mathrm{s},\\
\text{Rate limit:}\quad
&|\Delta \alpha| \le \rho\,dt, \qquad \rho = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.
\end{align}

The hysteresis band exists because a raw value hovering near a single
threshold would make authority chatter at the control rate. That is
physically absurd for a wheelchair, and --- more importantly for this
report --- it would make the frozen-$\alpha$ analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:math} meaningless.
\texttt{test\_adaptive\_hysteresis\_prevents\_chatter} drives values
through the band and asserts no toggling.

\subsubsection*{The safety filter: projecting instead of averaging}

The sixth variant does not blend at all. Below the intervention
threshold ($\ttc > 1.5$\,s) it returns $\ubci$ \emph{unchanged}, so it is
completely transparent when nothing is wrong. Above the threshold it
solves

\begin{equation}
u^{\star} = \arg\min_{u \in \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}}
\;\left(\frac{v - v_{\mathrm{bci}}}{v_{\max}}\right)^{2}
+ \left(\frac{\omega - \omega_{\mathrm{bci}}}{\omega_{\max}}\right)^{2},
\label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ is the feasible set of the dynamic
window. The result is therefore always \emph{some command the user could
have given}, never a compromise between two incompatible ones. The
metric is limit-normalised because m/s and rad/s are not comparable, and
an unnormalised norm would silently weight whichever axis happens to
carry larger numbers.

When every candidate is infeasible the planner degrades gracefully to
the maximum-clearance option rather than returning nothing, so the
filter always has a safe set to project onto.

\subsubsection*{Effective authority, for comparability}

The safety filter has no $\alpha$ of its own, but the ablations need
\texttt{mean\_alpha} to be comparable across all six variants. It
therefore reports the least-squares projection of its realised command
onto the segment between $\uauto$ and $\ubci$:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{eff}} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(
\frac{(u - \uauto)^{\!\top}(\ubci - \uauto)}
     {\lVert \ubci - \uauto\rVert^2}, \; 0,\, 1\right),
\label{eq:alphaeff}
\end{equation}

that is, the blend weight that \emph{would} have produced the same
command.

\subsection{A finding: some states are unrecoverable}
\label{sec:unrecoverable}

Two tests written during Stage 5 failed, and investigation showed the
tests' premises were wrong rather than the code. The finding is
quantitative and it justifies a design decision:

\begin{quote}
At $0.6$\,m from a $1.6$\,m-wide pillar travelling at $0.8$\,m/s, the
tightest turn available inside the dynamic window (slowest speed
$0.8 - 0.25 = 0.55$\,m/s, fastest yaw rate $0.75$\,rad/s) has radius
$0.55/0.75 = 0.73$\,m; after $0.6$\,m of forward travel such an arc has
moved only about $0.3$\,m sideways, far short of the $\approx 1.2$\,m
needed to clear the pillar.
\textbf{All 147 candidates are infeasible.} The chair is committed
before it arrives.
\end{quote}

This is the quantitative justification for triggering the safety filter
on \emph{time}-to-collision ($1.5$\,s ahead) rather than on proximity: by
the time proximity is alarming, intervention is already too late. It
may also contribute to the safety filter's residual collisions
(Section~\ref{sec:negative}), although that link was not measured.

\section[Mathematical Analysis: the Transport-Delay Stability Boundary]{Mathematical Analysis:\\ the Transport-Delay Stability Boundary}
\label{sec:math}
% =====================================================================

This section derives $\tau_{\max}(\alpha)$ --- the largest loop delay
the blended closed loop tolerates at a given authority --- exactly, with
no approximation of the delay.

\subsection{The characteristic equation}

Take one axis of the double integrator \eqref{eq:di} in error
coordinates, with the autonomous LQR acting on the \emph{true} state and
the operator's PD acting through the total loop delay $\tau$. Blending
with authority $\alpha$ per \eqref{eq:arb}:

\begin{equation}
\ddot p + d\,\dot p
+ (1-\alpha)\bigl(K_1 p + K_2 \dot p\bigr)
+ \alpha\bigl(K_p\, p(t-\tau) + K_d\, \dot p(t-\tau)\bigr) = 0 .
\label{eq:dde}
\end{equation}

Laplace transforming, and using
$\mathcal{L}\{p(t-\tau)\} = e^{-\tau s}P(s)$, gives the characteristic
equation

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\Delta(s) \;=\; s^{2} + d\,s + (1-\alpha)(K_1 + K_2 s)
+ \alpha (K_p + K_d s)\,e^{-\tau s} \;=\; 0. \;}
\label{eq:chareq}
\end{equation}

The factor $e^{-\tau s}$ makes \eqref{eq:chareq} \emph{transcendental}:
it has infinitely many roots and no closed-form root expression. What we
can do exactly is find where a root \emph{crosses} the imaginary axis,
because the delay-free loop ($\tau = 0$) is stable for every
$\alpha \in [0,1]$ --- all its polynomial coefficients are positive ---
and roots move continuously in $\tau$. The first crossing encountered as
$\tau$ grows from zero is therefore the boundary.

\subsection{Substituting \texorpdfstring{$s = j\omega$}{s = j omega}}

Put $s = j\omega$ with $\omega > 0$ and write $\theta = \omega\tau$.
Define

\begin{equation}
A = (1-\alpha)K_1 - \omega^{2},
\quad
B = \bigl(d + (1-\alpha)K_2\bigr)\omega,
\quad
C = \alpha K_p,
\quad
D = \alpha K_d \omega .
\label{eq:ABCD}
\end{equation}

Since $e^{-j\theta} = \cos\theta - j\sin\theta$,
equation~\eqref{eq:chareq} becomes
$(A + jB) + (C + jD)(\cos\theta - j\sin\theta) = 0$, and expanding the
product,

\begin{align}
\text{Real:}&\qquad A + C\cos\theta + D\sin\theta = 0, \label{eq:real}\\
\text{Imag:}&\qquad B + D\cos\theta - C\sin\theta = 0. \label{eq:imag}
\end{align}

\subsection{Solving for \texorpdfstring{$\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$}{cos theta and sin theta}}

Equations \eqref{eq:real}--\eqref{eq:imag} are linear in
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$:

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} C & D \\ D & -C \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} -A \\ -B \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det = -(C^2 + D^2).
\end{equation}

By Cramer's rule, provided $C^2 + D^2 \neq 0$ (i.e.\ $\alpha > 0$),

\begin{equation}
\cos\theta = -\frac{AC + BD}{C^{2}+D^{2}},
\qquad
\sin\theta = \frac{BC - AD}{C^{2}+D^{2}}.
\label{eq:cossin}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The delay cancels}

Now impose the Pythagorean identity. Substituting \eqref{eq:cossin} into
$\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ and clearing the denominator:

\begin{align}
(AC + BD)^2 + (BC - AD)^2 &= (C^2 + D^2)^2 \nonumber\\
A^2C^2 + \cancel{2ABCD} + B^2D^2 + B^2C^2 - \cancel{2ABCD} + A^2D^2
&= (C^2+D^2)^2 \nonumber\\
(A^2 + B^2)(C^2 + D^2) &= (C^2+D^2)^2,
\end{align}

and dividing by $C^2 + D^2 > 0$ leaves the \emph{magnitude balance}

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;A^{2} + B^{2} \;=\; C^{2} + D^{2}.\;}
\label{eq:magbalance}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[The interpretation]
The cross terms cancel identically, and with them every appearance of
$\tau$. This is not an algebraic accident but a statement of what a pure
transport delay does: $e^{-j\omega\tau}$ has unit modulus, so the delay
can \emph{rotate} the operator's contribution in the complex plane but
can never \emph{rescale} it. Whether a crossing exists at all is
therefore a question about magnitudes alone, and the magnitudes also fix
the crossing frequencies $\omega_i$. The delay only decides \emph{at
which delays}, $\tau_i + 2\pi k/\omega_i$, the phase lines up so that a
root sits on the axis.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A quadratic in \texorpdfstring{$\omega^2$}{omega squared}}

Introduce
\begin{equation}
P_1 = (1-\alpha)K_1, \quad
P_2 = d + (1-\alpha)K_2, \quad
Q_1 = \alpha K_p, \quad
Q_2 = \alpha K_d ,
\end{equation}
so that $A = P_1 - \omega^2$, $B = P_2\omega$, $C = Q_1$,
$D = Q_2\omega$. Expanding \eqref{eq:magbalance}:

\begin{equation}
\omega^{4} - 2P_1\omega^{2} + P_1^{2} + P_2^{2}\omega^{2}
= Q_1^{2} + Q_2^{2}\omega^{2},
\end{equation}

and collecting in $X = \omega^{2}$,

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
X^{2} + \underbrace{\bigl(P_2^{2} - Q_2^{2} - 2P_1\bigr)}_{b}\,X
+ \underbrace{\bigl(P_1^{2} - Q_1^{2}\bigr)}_{c} \;=\; 0. \;}
\label{eq:quadratic}
\end{equation}

Here $b$ and $c$ are just the quadratic's coefficients; $c$ is
unrelated to the decoder confidence $c$ of Section~\ref{sec:control}.
Every strictly positive real root $X_i$ of \eqref{eq:quadratic} gives a
crossing frequency $\omega_i = \sqrt{X_i}$. This is the entire
derivation: an infinite-dimensional stability question has been reduced
to a scalar quadratic.

\subsection{Recovering the delay}

For each crossing frequency, back-substitute into \eqref{eq:cossin} and
recover the phase:

\begin{equation}
\theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}\bigl(\sin\theta_i, \cos\theta_i\bigr)
\;\;\text{wrapped into}\;\; [0, 2\pi),
\qquad
\tau_i = \frac{\theta_i}{\omega_i}.
\label{eq:taui}
\end{equation}

The wrap into $[0, 2\pi)$ is what selects the \emph{smallest positive}
delay at which that particular frequency crosses; larger delays
$\tau_i + 2\pi k/\omega_i$ also cross but are not the first. Since the
loop is stable at $\tau = 0$, the boundary is the first crossing that
moves roots \emph{into} the right half-plane (Section~\ref{sec:direction}):

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\tau_{\max}(\alpha) = \min_{i\,:\,F'(X_i) > 0} \tau_i,\;}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\tau_{\max}(\alpha) = \infty
\text{ if \eqref{eq:quadratic} has no positive real root.}
\label{eq:taumax}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Direction of crossing, and stability switches}
\label{sec:direction}

A root pair can cross the imaginary axis in either direction, so a
crossing is not automatically a loss of stability. The direction is
settled by a standard result for characteristic equations of the form
$\mathcal{P}(s) + \mathcal{Q}(s)\,e^{-s\tau} = 0$~\cite{cooke1986zeroes}.
Here $\mathcal{P}(s) = s^2 + P_2 s + P_1$ is the undelayed part of
\eqref{eq:chareq} and $\mathcal{Q}(s) = Q_1 + Q_2 s$ the delayed part
(calligraphic letters, to avoid a clash with the Laplace transform
$P(s)$ of $p$ used in deriving \eqref{eq:chareq}), and the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:quadratic} is exactly
$F(\omega^2) = |\mathcal{P}(j\omega)|^2 - |\mathcal{Q}(j\omega)|^2$,
where $F(X) = X^2 + bX + c$. At a crossing $s = j\omega_i$,

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sign}\,\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{ds}{d\tau}\bigg|_{s = j\omega_i}
= \operatorname{sign} F'(X_i)
= \operatorname{sign}\,(2X_i + b).
\label{eq:direction}
\end{equation}

The larger root of \eqref{eq:quadratic} has $2X + b = \sqrt{b^2 - 4c} > 0$,
so it always \emph{destabilises}; a smaller positive root, which exists
only when $c > 0$ and $b < 0$, has $2X + b < 0$ and moves roots back to
the left. Two consequences follow for the gains of this study.

\begin{itemize}
\item For $\alpha \ge \astar$ we have $c \le 0$, so
      \eqref{eq:quadratic} has exactly one strictly positive root and
      every crossing is destabilising. Each adds a pair of right-half-plane
      roots, so the loop is stable \emph{if and only if}
      $\tau < \tau_{\max}(\alpha)$. Below $\alpha = 0.441425$ there is no
      crossing at all (Section~\ref{sec:alphastar}). So, outside the thin
      band $0.441425 \le \alpha < \astar$ described next, ``stable if
      and only if $\tau < \tau_{\max}(\alpha)$'' holds at every
      authority.
\item In the thin band $0.441425 \le \alpha < \astar = 0.441518$, both
      roots are positive (at the left end they coincide). The destabilising crossing still comes first,
      so $\tau_{\max}$ is unchanged, but \emph{stability switches} occur
      at larger delays. At $\alpha = 0.44146$, for example, the loop
      loses stability at $\tau = 10.54$\,s, regains it at $22.23$\,s and
      loses it again at $32.96$\,s. An independent computation of the
      rightmost characteristic root (Chebyshev pseudospectral
      discretisation of the delay equation) agrees with this sequence.
      The band is less than $10^{-4}$ wide and no result in this report
      depends on it.
\end{itemize}

Because the loop is stable at $\tau = 0$, there are no right-half-plane
roots for a stabilising crossing to remove, so the first crossing is
necessarily destabilising and \eqref{eq:taumax} always equals the
minimum over all crossings; the direction test matters only for what
happens at larger delays. All figures in this subsection are reproduced by
\texttt{python -m scripts.check\_crossing\_direction}.

\subsection{The delay-independent region and when \texorpdfstring{$\astar$}{alpha*} is exact}
\label{sec:alphastar}

Equation~\eqref{eq:quadratic} has no positive real root --- and the loop
is then stable at \emph{arbitrarily large} delay --- precisely when
either $c > 0$ and $b \ge 0$, or $b^2 < 4c$ (no real roots), or $c = 0$
and $b \ge 0$. In every case $c \ge 0$ is necessary, and since
$P_1, Q_1 \ge 0$,

\begin{equation}
c > 0
\iff P_1 > Q_1
\iff (1-\alpha)K_1 > \alpha K_p
\iff \alpha < \astar \equiv \frac{K_1}{K_1 + K_p}.
\label{eq:alphastar}
\end{equation}

So $\astar$ always \emph{upper-bounds} the delay-independent region.
Whether it is also the true boundary has a clean answer.

\begin{proposition}[Exactness criterion]
$\astar$ is the exact supremum of the delay-independent authorities if
and only if $b(\astar) \ge 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At $\alpha = \astar$ the constant term vanishes, $c = 0$, so
\eqref{eq:quadratic} factors as $X(X + b) = 0$ with roots
$\{0,\, -b\}$. If $b(\astar) \ge 0$ then $-b \le 0$ and there is no
strictly positive root, so the loop is delay-independent \emph{at}
$\astar$ and, by continuity, on an interval just below it; since $c < 0$
(a crossing exists) for every $\alpha > \astar$, $\astar$ is the
supremum. If $b(\astar) < 0$ then $-b > 0$ is a positive root, a
crossing exists at $\astar$, and the true boundary lies strictly below.
\end{proof}

Written out in the gains alone, the criterion is

\begin{equation}
\left(d + \frac{K_2 K_p}{K_1 + K_p}\right)^{2}
\;\ge\;
\left(\frac{K_d K_1}{K_1 + K_p}\right)^{2}
+ \frac{2 K_1 K_p}{K_1 + K_p}.
\label{eq:criterion}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[This corrects an earlier claim of ours]
We initially asserted that $\astar$ was exact. A $20{,}001$-point scan
of the authority axis disproved it, and the criterion
\eqref{eq:criterion} is the corrected result. For the gains of this
study, $b(\astar) = -0.0976 < 0$, so $\astar = 0.4415184$ is an
\emph{upper bound}; the true delay-independent boundary, located by
bisection on the exact predicate, is $0.4414251$. The bound
over-states the safe region by $0.021\%$ --- small, but the difference
between a bound and a boundary is not a matter of size.

The boundary is also approached in an unexpected way. As $\alpha$
decreases toward $0.441425$ from above, $\tau_{\max}(\alpha)$ does
\emph{not} grow without limit: it rises steeply to about $13.6$\,s and
then jumps to $\infty$. The jump happens because, at that authority, the
two positive roots of \eqref{eq:quadratic} merge ($b^2 = 4c$) and
disappear, so no crossing frequency is left. A plot whose delay axis
stops at a few seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}) makes this look like
a vertical asymptote; it is a finite jump.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The unicycle heading loop}
\label{sec:headingloop}

The analysis above is for the double integrator. The unicycle's heading
channel can be brought into the same form. Linearising about steady
forward motion, and using the yaw-rate lag from \eqref{eq:uni}, the
heading loop is

\begin{equation}
\tau_\omega\,\ddot\theta + \dot\theta = \omega_{\mathrm{cmd}} .
\label{eq:heading}
\end{equation}

Dividing through by $\tau_\omega$ puts it in exactly the form of
\eqref{eq:di} with effective damping $d = 1/\tau_\omega$ and every gain
scaled by $1/\tau_\omega$, so the identical machinery applies. With
$\tau_\omega = 0.20$\,s this gives $\astar = 0.6126$ and
$\tau_{\max}(1) = 0.647$\,s. For the heading loop the operator
derivative gain is $K_d = 0$ and criterion \eqref{eq:criterion} gives
$b(\astar) = 62.6 > 0$ (mainly because the effective damping
$d = 1/\tau_\omega = 5$ is large), so \textbf{$\astar$ is exact} there.
For this purpose the autonomous yaw law is represented by the
double-integrator LQR gains $K_1, K_2$ acting on heading error and yaw
rate. That is a surrogate --- the simulated chair's autonomy is DWA,
which has no linear gains --- and $\astar = 0.6126$ depends directly on
that choice.

We are explicit about the scope of this result in
Section~\ref{sec:limits}: it is a linearisation of one channel of the
unicycle about steady motion, and it does not cover the coupled
nonlinear $(v, \theta)$ dynamics, the speed gate \eqref{eq:intent}, or
obstacle interaction.

\subsection{The Pad\'e(2,2) comparison}
\label{sec:pade}

A common engineering shortcut replaces the delay with a rational
approximation,

\begin{equation}
e^{-\tau s} \;\approx\;
\frac{1 - \tau s/2 + (\tau s)^{2}/12}{1 + \tau s/2 + (\tau s)^{2}/12},
\label{eq:pade}
\end{equation}

turning \eqref{eq:chareq} into a quartic polynomial whose stability can
be assessed by ordinary root finding. Since the exact transcendental
solution is available here, Pad\'e is reported as a \emph{comparison},
not used as the method. Its margin is found by bisection on the largest
real part of the quartic's roots. Results are in
Table~\ref{tab:pade}; the approximation is consistently
\emph{optimistic}, which is the dangerous direction for a stability
bound.

\subsection{Direct DDE integration}
\label{sec:ddeint}

For verification we integrate \eqref{eq:dde} directly, with no reference
to the algebra above. Writing $z = p$, $w = \dot p$,
$c_z = (1-\alpha)K_1$, $c_w = d + (1-\alpha)K_2$:

\begin{equation}
\dot z = w,
\qquad
\dot w = -c_w w - c_z z - \alpha\bigl(K_p z(t-\tau) + K_d w(t-\tau)\bigr),
\end{equation}

with constant history $z(t) = z_0$, $w(t) = 0$ for $t < 0$.
Integration is fixed-step RK4 at $h = 2\times10^{-3}$\,s over a $60$\,s
horizon. The delayed state is required at $t - \tau$, which in general
falls \emph{between} grid points; we obtain it by cubic Hermite
interpolation,

\begin{equation}
y(s) = (2s^3 - 3s^2 + 1)y_k + (s^3 - 2s^2 + s)h\,\dot y_k
     + (-2s^3 + 3s^2)y_{k+1} + (s^3 - s^2)h\,\dot y_{k+1},
\label{eq:hermite}
\end{equation}

which is fourth-order between grid points and, importantly, means
$\tau$ need not be an integer multiple of $h$. (Derivative jumps from
the constant initial history limit the global order, but the error at
$h = 2\times10^{-3}$ is about $10^{-7}$, far below every tolerance
used.) A scheme that rounded
$\tau$ to the grid would quantise the very quantity being measured.

The dominant growth rate is estimated by comparing the response envelope
over two late windows,

\begin{equation}
\lambda = \frac{\ln E_{\text{late}} - \ln E_{\text{mid}}}
               {\bar t_{\text{late}} - \bar t_{\text{mid}}},
\qquad
E_{\text{mid}} = \max_{t \in [0.4T,\,0.6T]} |z(t)|,
\quad
E_{\text{late}} = \max_{t \in [0.8T,\,T]} |z(t)| ,
\label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}

so $\lambda < 0$ means settling and $\lambda > 0$ means diverging.

\subsection{DDE bisection}
\label{sec:bisection}

The third check recovers the boundary \emph{numerically}, consulting the
analytic formula only to choose a bracket. Given $\alpha$, bracket
$[\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}, \tau_{\mathrm{hi}}] = [10^{-3},\,
3\tau_{\max}^{\text{analytic}}]$ and bisect on the predicate
$\lambda(\alpha, \tau) < 0$:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{mid}} \leftarrow \tfrac12(\tau_{\mathrm{lo}} + \tau_{\mathrm{hi}}),
\qquad
\begin{cases}
\tau_{\mathrm{lo}} \leftarrow \tau_{\mathrm{mid}} & \text{if } \lambda < 0
\quad (\text{still stable}),\\
\tau_{\mathrm{hi}} \leftarrow \tau_{\mathrm{mid}} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

for $60$ iterations or until the bracket narrows below $10^{-5}$\,s. The
predicate is monotone in $\tau$ near the boundary, which is what makes
bisection valid. Each evaluation is a full $60$\,s DDE integration, so
this check is expensive --- and it is the strongest of the three,
because it recovers the number rather than merely testing a consequence
of it.

together with the fraction of decisions that were degraded.

\subsection{Computational cost and how it was reduced}

The first working version of the episode loop cost $1.6$\,s per episode,
putting the full grid at roughly $14$ hours. Two changes brought it to
approximately $72$\,ms:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Evaluate risk at the planning rate, not the simulation
      rate.} Risk depends on a rollout; recomputing it at $50$\,Hz when
      the plan itself only changes at $10$\,Hz was pure waste.
\item \textbf{Parallelise across 18 processes.} Episodes are independent
      by construction --- each has its own generator --- so this is
      exact rather than approximate.
\end{enumerate}

The full Stage 6 grid now runs in about $39$ minutes. Two earlier
optimisations were prerequisites: the closed-form arc rollout
(Section~\ref{sec:dwa}), which replaced $11{,}025$ integration steps per
control step with one vectorised expression, and the distance-transform
clearance lookup (Section~\ref{sec:plants}).

\subsection{Statistical treatment}

Because the variant is excluded from the RNG key, every policy sees an
identical noise realisation at a given seed, so comparisons are
\textbf{paired}. Episode-level means are reported with $95\%$
confidence half-widths (normal approximation) as \emph{descriptive}
summaries only. Inferential comparisons against \vadapt{} are made at
the level of the subject: paired differences in per-subject success,
with percentile bootstrap intervals over the nine subjects
(Table~\ref{tab:paired}; Section~\ref{sec:stats} explains why
episode-level tests would be pseudoreplication). Correlations between
per-subject success and decoder $\kappa$ are Pearson, with $n = 9$ --- a
sample size we treat with appropriate suspicion in
Section~\ref{sec:persubject}.

\begin{remark}[A frontier reading that the headline table hides]
\label{rem:frontier}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} reports \vadapt{} at a \emph{mean} authority of
$0.118$ with $0.996$ success. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} shows that a
\emph{constant} $\alpha = 0.1$ achieves $1.000$. On mean authority
alone, therefore, the adaptive policy sits essentially \emph{on} the
fixed-$\alpha$ frontier, not above it, and we would be overstating our
result to claim otherwise.

The defensible distinction is not how much authority is delivered in
total but \emph{when} it is delivered. A constant $\alpha = 0.1$ gives
the user a permanently attenuated command --- ninety percent of every
input is discarded, including in an empty corridor where obeying it
costs nothing. The adaptive law raises authority towards the decoder's
current confidence when confidence is high and the path is clear
($\alpha$ peaks at about $0.6$--$0.65$ in Figure~\ref{fig:alphatrace}), and lowers it towards
zero when risk rises or confidence is low. Figure~\ref{fig:alphatrace}
illustrates this in one episode; it is not a measurement of the
distribution, and the aggregate data are less favourable (mean $\alpha$
is $0.16$ even in the open corridor). A distribution-level metric ---
authority delivered per unit of risk --- is the measurement this study
lacks and that future work should add.
\end{remark}

\vadapt{}'s $r = -0.125$ ($p = 0.749$) shows no relationship with

sits at the ceiling.

Under \vadapt{} the \emph{authority} a subject receives does track

and is in effect driven by the autonomy, which also reaches $1.000$ for
every subject. The policy converts a poor decoder into \emph{less
authority} rather than into more collisions; it does not show that a
poor decoder's user is given meaningful control.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The averaging failure, demonstrated}
\label{sec:averaging}

The brief required this failure be \emph{shown} rather than asserted. It
is shown twice: once at the plant's command interface, before any
controller exists, and once in the full closed loop with actuator lag.

At the interface, two intents $40^\circ$ either side of an obstacle
convert through \eqref{eq:intent} to:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Command & $v_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ (m/s) & $\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ (rad/s) \\
\midrule
Operator, $40^\circ$ left  & 0.59 & $+1.40$ \\
Autonomy, $40^\circ$ right & 0.59 & $-1.40$ \\
\textbf{50/50 blend}       & \textbf{0.59} & \textbf{0.00} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Both inputs turn hard away from the obstacle; their average drives
straight into it at \emph{undiminished} speed. In the closed loop, with
the chair at $(3.0, 4.0)$ heading at the pillar at $0.6$\,m/s:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Policy & Collision & Min.\ clearance \\
\midrule
Operator alone, turn left ($\omega = +0.6$)  & no  & 0.453\,m \\
Autonomy alone, turn right ($\omega = -0.6$) & no  & 0.453\,m \\
\textbf{\vfixed{} 50/50}                     & \textbf{YES} & \textbf{0.300\,m} \\
\vsafety{}                                   & no  & 0.453\,m \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The collision radius is $0.35$\,m. Both commands are individually safe
by a comfortable margin; their average is not. The mechanism is exactly
that identified in Section~\ref{sec:intent}: the blend cancels the yaw
($0.5(+0.6) + 0.5(-0.6) = 0$) while forward speed survives untouched, so
the chair proceeds straight at precisely the obstacle both original
commands were avoiding.

This is the structural argument against convex blending as an
arbitration primitive, and it is why \vsafety{} \emph{projects} onto the
safe set \eqref{eq:filter} rather than averaging: a projection always
returns some command the user could have given, never a compromise
between two incompatible ones.

\subsection{A repair that had to come first}
\label{sec:repair}

The conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} --- that risk carries the
safety and measured confidence adds no measurable success --- was originally obtained from an experiment that
\emph{could not have concluded otherwise}, and is reported here only
after that was fixed.

In the original channel, each decision's confidence was drawn from a flat
pool of the subject's measured posteriors, independently of whether the
simulated command was actually wrong. Confidence and correctness were
therefore statistically independent \emph{by construction}. On the real
decoder the calibrated posterior separates correct from wrong trials at
$\mathrm{AUC} = 0.676$; the simulation reproduced $0.500$, which is pure
noise. Concluding ``confidence does not matter'' from that setup was
unfalsifiable.

The channel now draws confidence conditionally on the chain's state, which
restores the empirical relationship (simulated $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.679$),
and the entire grid was re-run. Because the conditional draw consumes the
generator identically, the two runs share seeds, chain states and
latencies, so they can be compared subject by subject:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
& $\Delta$ success & 95\% CI \\
\midrule
\vconf{}                 & $+0.002$ & $[-0.015,\ +0.017]$ \\
\vadapt{}                & $+0.000$ & $[-0.006,\ +0.006]$ \\
\vadapt{}, no risk term  & $-0.015$ & $[-0.056,\ +0.028]$ \\
\vmanual{}, \vfixed{}, \vsafety{} & exactly $0$ & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Nothing moved.} The three variants that never read confidence are
unchanged to the last digit, which is the check that the two runs really
are comparable; the confidence-dependent variants moved by amounts
indistinguishable from Monte-Carlo noise.

The conclusion therefore survives, but it now means something stronger and
different: not that an uninformative signal was ignored, but that
\emph{a genuinely informative signal was available and the controller
still gained nothing measurable from it}. The mechanism is that
conditional sampling preserves each subject's \emph{marginal} confidence
distribution --- mean $\alpha$ for \vconf{} is $0.387$ before and after ---
so the same total authority is handed out, merely better timed, and the
timing signal is too weak to matter. Within a subject the separation
$\mathbb{E}[c \mid \text{correct}] - \mathbb{E}[c \mid \text{wrong}]$
averages only $0.052$, and for four of nine subjects it is below $0.02$
(for subject 5 it is negative). The pooled figure of $0.68$ is largely a
\emph{between}-subject effect, and between-subject structure cannot help a
controller that only ever sees one subject at a time.

Four conclusions follow, and the first is uncomfortable.

\textbf{The risk term carries the safety; confidence governs how much
authority is returned.} Deleting risk costs \vadapt{} $20$ points of
success. Replacing the measured confidence with a \emph{perfect oracle}
--- one that knows whether each decision was actually correct --- leaves
success unchanged ($0.996$), but success is at its ceiling, so that null
says little; what the oracle does change is authority, which nearly
doubles (mean $\alpha$ $0.118 \rightarrow 0.230$) --- the one thing
\vadapt{} offers over \vauto{} (Section~\ref{sec:honest}). No ablation
removes confidence while keeping risk ($c \equiv 1$), so the study
cannot rank the two terms outright. It shows that risk is necessary for
safety, and that confidence decides how much authority can safely be
returned. Since the policy is named for confidence and it is risk that
keeps it safe, Section~\ref{sec:reframe} reframes it accordingly.

\textbf{A perfect confidence signal does help when it is all you have.}
\vconf{} gains $+0.19$ from an oracle. Note what that compares: a
\emph{perfect} signal against a real one. It says the ceiling is higher,
not that the real signal is informative enough to reach it. The
distinction matters for anyone designing a system without a map or a
range sensor, where risk cannot be computed.

\textbf{Age decay does not show that confidence is informative --- an
earlier draft read it backwards.} Age decay helps \vconf{} by $+0.25$
and leaves \vadapt{}'s success essentially unchanged ($0.996 \to 1.000$)
while cutting its mean authority
from $0.118$ to $0.029$. The earlier reading was that this proves
confidence carries information. It does not. Age decay pulls confidence
toward the prior $0.25$, which is \emph{below} the marginal mean of
$0.455$, so its dominant effect is simply to \textbf{lower $\alpha$} and
hand back less authority. Making a signal \emph{less} informative while
performance \emph{improves} is evidence against that signal's
usefulness, not for it. It is therefore \emph{reported} as a level
effect, not adopted as a default.

\textbf{Burst structure does not bind, and delay-induced instability
does not explain the failures.} Both ablations move nothing --- but for
delay, the ablation is not the reason we can say so.
$\texttt{no\_delay}$ removes $\tau_h = 0.2$\,s from a $3.77$\,s loop
delay (the \vmanual{} value; $3.84$\,s pooled), that is $5\%$ of it, so
its null establishes nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}). What can be said is narrower: $97\%$ of
the degradation across the authority sweep occurs below the heading-loop
bound $\astar = 0.6126$ (Section~\ref{sec:headingloop}), where even the
linearised delayed-feedback model would be \emph{delay-independent}
--- stable for any delay whatever. Together with the obstacle-free run of
Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}, which shows no instability even $5.8\times$
past the boundary, this makes delay-induced \emph{instability} an
unlikely cause of the failures (the linear model is only a reference for
the simulated chair); it does not show that
latency has no effect on performance (decoder latency was never
ablated, and in free space delay alone adds $29\%$ to the time to goal,
Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}). Bursts do not bind because there are
none in this dataset (Section~\ref{sec:markov}).

Since the dataset does not exhibit burst errors, the intention was to
establish whether the conclusion would survive if it did. \textbf{This
experiment cannot establish that, and the robustness claim an earlier
draft made here has been withdrawn.}

The reason is a resolution limit. In the \texttt{main} condition an
episode issues a median of $5$ decisions under \vadapt{} and $4$ under
\vfixed{}. At $4.0\times$ the
imposed mean burst reaches $20.95$ decisions for subject 5. A burst of
$21$ cannot occur inside an episode of $5$, so for the two variants that
matter the independent variable is not expressible at the upper end of
its own range. \vadapt{} is flat across the sweep, but that cannot be
read as robustness: \vadapt{} is at its ceiling, and long bursts cannot
be realised within an episode.

Both \vfixed{} and \vsafety{} move with clustering by similar amounts up
to $2.5\times$ ($0.178 \to 0.230$ and $0.357 \to 0.407$), although only
\vsafety{} has many decisions per episode (median $30$ in the
\texttt{main} condition, because its episodes last ${\sim}45$\,s).
Clustering therefore does act even within short episodes --- a burst
longer than an episode makes that episode's errors all-or-nothing ---
so the resolution argument explains less than it first appears to. With
one sweep and a non-monotonic final point these are observations, not
results.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The honest caveat, stated first}
\label{sec:honest}

\textbf{The adaptive policy is not significantly better than simply
handing over to the autonomy} ($\Delta = -0.004$, 95\% CI
$[-0.009,\ +0.000]$ over nine subjects), and it is slower ($11.7$\,s
against $10.5$\,s). On every task-performance metric
(success, collisions, time), \vauto{} is at least as good as \vadapt{}.

We state this before the positive claims because the alternative --- a
report that leads with $99.6\%$ and mentions the comparison later --- is
the kind of presentation that invites a reader to discover the caveat
themselves and discount everything around it.

What the adaptive policy buys is that it \emph{returns $12\%$ of
authority to the user at a small cost in safety}: $2$ collisions in
$540$ episodes against none, and a success difference whose
subject-level $95\%$ interval $[-0.009, +0.000]$ lies above the margin
$-\delta_s = -0.02$ (Section~\ref{sec:stats}), so \vadapt{} is
non-inferior to \vauto{} at this simulation-resolution margin, and never
better. How much authority is returned depends strongly on the decoder:
about $3\%$ for the four subjects with the lowest confidence, and
between about $10\%$ and $30\%$ for the other five ($0.30$ for
subject 3). Whether that is
worth anything depends on user agency and acceptance, which this study
does not measure. So the defensible claim is narrower than the headline
number, and still useful:

\begin{quote}
\itshape
In simulation, authority can be returned to a user whose decoder is
wrong 56\% of the time without giving up, within the resolution of this
experiment, the safety of full autonomy --- provided the arbitration is
conditioned on situational risk.
\end{quote}

There is a real argument for why returning authority matters, and it is
worth stating even though this study cannot test it: a wheelchair that
ignores its user is a wheelchair the user is a passenger in. The
literature on assistive robotics is consistent that perceived agency
drives acceptance, and a device that is safe but not felt to be
controlled is a device that is abandoned. But that is a claim about
users, and it requires a user study, not a simulation.

\subsection{Reframing: risk-gated authority, not confidence-adaptive control}
\label{sec:reframe}

The ablations in Section~\ref{sec:ablations} do not support the name we
started with. The law $\alpha = c\,(1-r)$ was conceived as a
\emph{confidence}-adaptive policy with a risk correction. The evidence
says that risk, not confidence, is what keeps it safe:

\begin{itemize}
\item Removing $r$ costs $20$ points of success.
\item Replacing $c$ with a perfect oracle leaves success unchanged (it
      is at its ceiling) but raises mean authority from $0.118$ to
      $0.230$.
\end{itemize}

The correct description of the mechanism is therefore \textbf{risk-gated
authority}: the controller withholds authority when obeying the user
would be consequential. Risk decides \emph{when} authority must be
withdrawn; confidence decides \emph{how much} can be returned in the
first place.

This reframing has a practical consequence that strengthens rather than
weakens the contribution. A risk signal requires a map and a range
sensor but \emph{no} decoder introspection; a confidence signal requires
a calibrated probabilistic decoder. If risk carries the safety, the approach
may transfer to BCIs and assistive interfaces that emit no usable
confidence at all --- a much larger class of devices. With $c \equiv 1$
the law becomes $\alpha = 1 - r$. The
$\vconf{}$ results show what remains achievable when only confidence is
available ($0.31$, rising to $0.57$ with age decay). Without the risk
term the gated law reaches $0.80$; no risk-only variant ($c \equiv 1$)
was run, so what risk achieves without confidence is not measured.

\subsection{Why blending falls off the frontier}

Section~\ref{sec:averaging} gives the mechanism, and it is worth stating
in general terms because it is not specific to wheelchairs.

Convex blending assumes the average of two good commands is a good
command. That assumption holds when the safe set is convex in the input
space. For a nonholonomic vehicle it is \emph{not}: the set of
collision-free $(v, \omega)$ around an obstacle is generally disconnected
or non-convex, with one component turning left and another turning
right. The midpoint of two points in different components need not lie
in the set at all --- and equation~\eqref{eq:intent} guarantees that
here the midpoint retains full forward speed while cancelling the yaw,
so it lies in the worst part of the complement.

This is one reason \vfixed{} sits at $0.161$ despite receiving a
perfectly good autonomous command half the time. It is not the only
one: \vfixed{} also fails $60\%$ of corridor episodes, where there is
nothing to average around, and the operator's own command is unsafe near
obstacles (Section~\ref{sec:operator}). The mechanism is also why the
safety filter is constructed as a \emph{projection} \eqref{eq:filter}. A projection onto
the safe set returns a point \emph{in} the set by definition, whatever
the set's shape.

The practical lesson is a design rule: where the safe set may be
non-convex, do not average commands of \emph{comparable weight} near an
obstacle. The policies that succeed here either keep the user's weight
small (a constant $\alpha \le 0.1$ reaches $1.000$) or, like \vadapt{},
drive it towards zero as risk rises --- close to selecting the
autonomous command. Projection is the principled alternative, but as
implemented here (\vsafety{}) it was safe without being effective
(Section~\ref{sec:negative}).

\subsection{Two negative results, reported as such}
\label{sec:negative}

\textbf{Decoder errors do not arrive in bursts on this dataset}
(Section~\ref{sec:markov}). Zero of nine subjects show significant burst
structure. We report the null rather than the model, having first
demonstrated that the test detects real bursts at $p<0.0001$, and having
explained the null structurally through the dataset's randomised cue
order. The Markov machinery is retained --- it is the right model if
bursts exist --- and burstiness became a swept variable. That sweep
cannot resolve long bursts within a five-decision episode
(Section~\ref{sec:burstresults}), so robustness to genuinely bursty
errors remains an open question rather than a result.

\textbf{The minimum-intervention safety filter is safe but not
helpful.} It cuts collisions sevenfold against fixed blending
($0.120$ vs.\ $0.839$) while preserving the most user authority of any
non-manual policy ($\alpha = 0.771$). But it succeeds only $35.6\%$ of
the time and its mean episode lasts $44.8$\,s: it prevents crashes
without enabling progress. Most failures are timeouts ($52\%$ of
episodes, $94\%$ in the cluttered room) rather than collisions
($12\%$): staying as close as possible to an operator command that
points at an obstacle leaves the chair stalled in front of it
(Figure~\ref{fig:paths}). The residual collisions are consistent with, but
not shown to be caused by, the committed states of
Section~\ref{sec:unrecoverable}, in which no feasible command avoids the
obstacle; intervening earlier would mean intervening more, which is
precisely what minimum intervention is designed not to do.

This is a genuine tension in the minimum-intervention philosophy, not an
implementation defect: the threshold that keeps the filter unobtrusive
also limits how early it can act.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The stability proof does not cover the demonstration plant}
\label{sec:proofscope}

This is the most important boundary to draw, and we draw it explicitly.

The exact boundary of Section~\ref{sec:math} is derived for a
\textbf{linear double integrator}, one axis, with an undelayed LQR and a
delayed PD operator, at \textbf{frozen} $\alpha$. The wheelchair results
of Section~\ref{sec:results} are produced on a \textbf{nonlinear,
nonholonomic unicycle with actuator lag, obstacles, and time-varying}
$\alpha$. These are not the same system, and no claim in this report
should be read as proving stability of the second from analysis of the
first.

What the analysis does provide for the unicycle is the heading-loop
recast of Section~\ref{sec:headingloop}: linearised about steady forward
motion, the yaw channel maps exactly onto the same machinery, giving
$\astar = 0.6126$ (exact there, since $K_d = 0$) and
$\tau_{\max}(1) = 0.647$\,s. It is a result about a linear heading
loop with an LQR-type autonomy (a surrogate for DWA) and delayed heading
feedback; the simulated chair has neither (Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}),
so the recast is a reference model, not an analysis of the simulated
channel. It says nothing about the coupled $(v,\theta)$
dynamics, about the speed gate \eqref{eq:intent}, which is nonlinear by
construction, or about the switching introduced by obstacle proximity.

Three specific gaps follow:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Frozen-$\alpha$ analysis of a time-varying system.} The
adaptive policy varies $\alpha$ online. Hysteresis, rate limiting and
the $0.25$\,s lag remove chatter, and Figure~\ref{fig:alphatrace} shows
the trace is smooth. They do not make $\alpha$ slow relative to the
dynamics: the filter constants ($0.25$--$0.30$\,s) are no longer than
the loop's dominant time constants (about $0.3$--$0.85$\,s), so there
is no time-scale separation to appeal to. Even for the linear model the
frozen-$\alpha$ boundary is therefore only a heuristic \emph{guide} for
a time-varying $\alpha$, not a proof; for the simulated wheelchair it
is not a guide at all (item 3). A
rigorous treatment needs a switched-system argument (common Lyapunov
function or dwell-time condition) or an LPV formulation with $\alpha$ as
the scheduling parameter. We consider this the single most valuable
piece of future theoretical work.

\item \textbf{The DWA layer is not covered at all.} It is a receding-horizon
optimiser with a nonconvex feasible set. Nothing in
Section~\ref{sec:math} applies to it.

\item \textbf{Delay was not the binding constraint,} but not because the
loop had margin --- it has none. The effective loop delay under \vmanual{}
is $3.77$\,s ($3.84$\,s pooled) against a heading-loop margin of
$0.647$\,s, which is $5.8\times$ past the boundary, and the loop still
does not destabilise. The reason is a
structural mismatch between the loop analysed and the loop simulated,
set out in Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}. The stability analysis is
therefore a rigorous result about a loop the simulator does not
implement: the experiments neither validate nor stress it, and the four
verification checks remain its only support.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The delay boundary does not transfer to the simulator}
\label{sec:delaytruth}

An earlier draft of this report stated that the loop ran at $38\%$ of
its delay margin, comparing $\tau_h = 0.2$\,s against $\tau_{\max}(1) =
0.529$\,s. Both numbers were the wrong ones. The correction is recorded
here rather than made silently, because the conclusion it supported
turns out to be right for a different and better reason.

\textbf{The delay was under-counted.} The boundary constrains the
\emph{total} loop delay. The BCI channel samples the operator's intent
once per $T = 2$\,s, holds it, and transports it for a further
$2$--$3$\,s, and every part of that lies inside the outer position loop (the
operator steers from where it last saw the chair).
Measured directly from \texttt{ChannelOutput.age\_s} over $319{,}345$
simulation steps, the mean age of the active command is $3.64$\,s, so
$\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} = \tau_h + \mathrm{age} = 3.84$\,s. The
measured age agrees with the standard sampled-data treatment, which adds
$T/2$ to the mean transport delay: $2.64 + 1.0 \approx 3.64$\,s, using
the mean sampled latency of about $2.64$\,s. One consequence is immediate ---
the \texttt{no\_delay} ablation removes $5\%$ of the loop delay, so its
null result establishes nothing in either direction.

$3.84$\,s is moreover a \textbf{lower} bound. The latency estimator's
horizon is $4.0$\,s, but \texttt{latency.max\_s} clips sampling at
$3.0$\,s, and censoring is severe --- every trial of subject 4, $99\%$ of
subject 9's and $92\%$ of subject 5's terminate at the horizon. Those
trials are simulated a full second faster than they were measured, so the
true loop delay is further past the boundary than the figures here, never
nearer.

\textbf{The margin was the wrong plant's.} $0.529$\,s is
$\tau_{\max}(1)$ for the double integrator. The plant simulated is the
unicycle, whose heading loop (Section~\ref{sec:headingloop}) gives
$\astar = 0.6126$ and $\tau_{\max}(1) = 0.647$\,s.

Corrected, \vmanual{} ($\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.77$\,s) runs at $5.8\times$ the boundary rather than at
$38\%$ of it. \textbf{And it does not destabilise.} Removing every
obstacle, so a failure cannot be a collision, and forcing every command
correct, so a failure cannot be decoder error, leaves only the delayed
loop itself:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
$\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}$ & regime & goal reached & time to goal \\
\midrule
$3.77$\,s & $5.8\times$ past & $1.000$ & $11.21$\,s \\
$1.98$\,s & $3.1\times$ past & $1.000$ & $9.95$\,s \\
$0.72$\,s & $1.1\times$ past & $1.000$ & $9.04$\,s \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Delay costs $29\%$ in time-to-goal and nothing else: no oscillation, no
divergence, no failure. Two features of the simulator explain it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Delayed reference, undelayed feedback.} The command is
formed from the operator's delayed \emph{position} but the chair's
\emph{current} heading, so the yaw law is $\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}} =
k_\theta\,(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}(t-\tau) - \theta(t))$, whereas Stage 7
analyses $K_p\,(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}} - \theta(t-\tau))$. For the heading channel
alone, with $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ treated as an external signal, a
delay on the reference does not enter the characteristic equation
($\tau_\omega s^2 + s + k_\theta = 0$) and cannot destabilise that loop.
$\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is, however, formed from the delayed position,
so the delay does sit in the outer position loop, which this report does
not analyse; that loop's gain is roughly $v/(\text{distance to goal})$,
small except near the goal, which is the likely reason no instability
appears. This is not a defect in the simulator --- a real chair's yaw
controller reads its own gyro, not a delayed estimate --- but it does
mean the boundary does not apply to the channel it was derived for.

\item \textbf{Saturation.} $v_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ sits at $v_{\max}$ in
$77.4\%$ of steps. This does not bear on the heading-loop boundary
directly, but it caps the gain of the outer position loop through which
the delay acts (item 1).
\end{enumerate}

What survives is the negative result, on firmer ground than the margin
ratio it replaces: $97\%$ of the degradation across the authority sweep
occurs at $\alpha < \astar = 0.6126$ (the heading-loop bound), where even
the linearised delayed-feedback model has $\tau_{\max} = \infty$, so
\emph{no} delay of any magnitude can destabilise it. Together with
the obstacle-free run above, this makes delay-induced instability an
unlikely cause of these failures (the linear model is only a reference
for the simulated chair), and that no longer rests on an ablation which moves $5\%$ of the quantity in question;
whether latency degrades performance in other ways, for example near
obstacles, is not tested. Everything above is reproduced by
\texttt{python -m scripts.audit\_delay\_margin}.

\subsection{The dataset substitution: discrete classes, continuous commands}

As set out in Section~\ref{sec:modality}, $\ubci$ is a continuous
delayed-PD command modulated by measured decoder statistics, not one of
four discrete symbols. The substitution buys a continuous command
value, which the linear analysis needs; it does not buy exact
correspondence between the analysed loop and the simulated loop
(Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}). It costs realism in a specific and
identifiable way.

What is \emph{measured} from real EEG: error rate, per-class confusion
structure, calibrated confidence, decision latency and its censoring.
What is \emph{not}: the piecewise-constant, four-symbol waveform that a
real MI-BCI actually emits. A real user issuing ``turn left'' receives a
sustained turn until the next decision, whereas our channel delivers a
continuously varying vector. The likely direction of the bias is that
our channel is \emph{smoother} and therefore somewhat easier to arbitrate
than a real one, which would make the reported success rates mildly
optimistic in absolute terms. Sharing the channel and the seeds does not
make the policies equally exposed: \vauto{} never uses the channel,
and policies that give the user more authority depend on it more. The
bias could therefore change the \emph{ranking}, most plausibly the
adaptive-versus-autonomy comparison (margin $0.004$); future-work item 3
is the test.

A discrete-command variant is the most valuable single extension to this
work, and the architecture already supports it: the modality is a
configuration switch, and the confusion sampler already produces
class-level decisions.

\subsection{The operator model}
\label{sec:operatorlimits}

The operator is a delayed PD controller that \emph{always knows the
goal}. Real users adapt, learn their decoder's quirks, change strategy
when it misbehaves, and --- critically --- react to having authority
taken away. There is no co-adaptation loop here, and a real user's
response to intervention is entirely outside the model. This is the
largest gap between the simulation and reality, larger than any plant
approximation.

It also interacts with the central claim. The argument for adaptive over
pure autonomy is that it returns authority; whether returning authority
to a \emph{simulated} PD controller means anything at all is exactly the
question a user study would answer and this study cannot.

The operator also has \textbf{no obstacle avoidance}: its intent points
straight at the goal (Section~\ref{sec:operator}). Two consequences
follow. First, the premise figure ($11.9\%$ success for \vmanual{})
measures an obstacle-blind driver as well as a noisy channel; only the
corridor isolates decoder error. Second, part of what the risk term does
here is withhold authority from a \emph{correctly decoded} command that
is itself unsafe --- a hazard that decoder confidence cannot detect by
construction. This favours risk over confidence in
Section~\ref{sec:ablations}; an obstacle-aware operator model could
change that balance.

\subsection{No sensitivity analysis}

Every headline number comes from one hand-set configuration: the Schmitt
thresholds ($0.60/0.40$), the risk horizon and floor ($3.0/0.5$\,s), the
DWA weights, and the operator gains and delay ($K_p = 4$, $K_d = 2$,
$\tau_h = 0.2$\,s), which were chosen rather than fitted to human data.
The gate threshold matters in particular: it sits above the mean
calibrated confidence ($0.455$), which is the likely reason four
subjects receive almost no authority. None of these parameters was swept ($\tau_h$ was only removed, in the
no-delay ablation), so the
adaptive policy's authority level, and possibly its safety, may not
generalise.

\subsection{Environment and sensing idealisations}

The environments are three hand-built 2-D occupancy grids with static
obstacles, a perfect map, and exact self-localisation. There are no
people, no doors, no dynamic obstacles, no odometry drift, no
localisation uncertainty, and no sensor faults (dropout probability is
configured to zero and kept there --- sensor faults are out of scope).
Range sensing has $2$\,cm of additive Gaussian noise, which is
optimistic.

The doorway environment is deliberately adversarial for blending, which
cuts both ways: it makes the averaging failure reproducible, and it also
means the aggregate collision figures for \vfixed{} are influenced by a
geometry chosen to defeat it. The corridor and cluttered environments
are included partly to keep that honest.

\subsection{Predictor--plant mismatch in DWA}

DWA rollouts assume the commanded velocity is achieved instantly, while
the plant reaches it through first-order lags $\tau_v = 0.30$\,s and
$\tau_\omega = 0.20$\,s. This is standard practice, and it is mitigated
two ways --- the dynamic window already restricts candidates to
velocities reachable under the acceleration limits, and the plan is
recomputed at $10$\,Hz so any prediction is trusted only briefly ---
but it is an approximation. Safety does not rest on rollout fidelity; it
rests on the continuously re-evaluated risk signal. Agreement between
the closed-form arc predictor and the RK4 plant integrator is pinned to
$10^{-6}$ over $1.5$\,s by test.

\subsection{Statistical caveats}
\label{sec:stats}

The unit of analysis is the \textbf{subject}, and there are nine of
them. The $540$ episodes behind each variant are $9$ subjects $\times\ 3$
environments $\times\ 20$ Monte-Carlo seeds; seeds are repetitions, not
participants. An earlier draft ran paired $t$-tests across all $540$ as
though they were independent observations, which is pseudoreplication,
and produced $p$-values as small as $10^{-248}$. Those have been
withdrawn and replaced by subject-level differences with bootstrap
intervals over the nine subjects. No statistical treatment makes $n = 9$
into a large sample.

The comparison against pure autonomy is a \textbf{non-inferiority}
claim, not an equivalence claim and not a null-hypothesis test. A test
would be useless here in any case: seven of the nine subject-level
differences are exactly zero, leaving two free signs, so an exact
sign-flip test has a smallest attainable two-sided $p$ of $0.5$. The
margin $\delta_s = 0.02$ was fixed before the final re-run from the
experiment's own resolution (not a pre-registration: earlier results had
already been seen) --- each subject contributes $60$ episodes, so $1/60 = 0.0167$
is the finest difference the design can express --- and is therefore an
\textbf{experiment-resolution tolerance supporting a simulation-level
statement only}. It is not a clinical, safety, or real-world
acceptability threshold, and must not be read as one.

The per-subject correlations of Section~\ref{sec:persubject} still have
$n = 9$ and are still reported with Pearson $r$; at that sample size this
is a weak instrument, \vadapt{}'s is uninformative because of the
ceiling, as already flagged, and it is the same parametric construction this section rejects
elsewhere. It is retained only because the qualitative claim ---
that adaptive decouples success from decoder quality --- does not rest on
the $p$-value.

Collision is terminal, so the collision metric is a rate rather than a
count, and episodes that time out are counted as failures rather than
analysed separately.

\subsection{The simulated channel is approximately, not exactly, calibrated}

Three quantities in the channel that ought to agree do not, in the third
decimal place. The chain's transitions use smoothed estimates; its
initial state is seeded from the stationary rate implied by the
\emph{unsmoothed} estimates; and the burstiness re-parameterisation and
the i.i.d.\ ablation both use the empirical marginal. The rate the
chain actually runs at differs from the measured rate by at most
$0.0044$ --- $0.44$ percentage points, for subject 5 ($0.8134$ against
$0.8090$); the rate used to seed the initial state differs by up to
$0.0066$ ($0.8156$ for the same subject).

This is disclosed rather than fixed: correcting it means refitting in
Stage 2 and revising every published Stage 2 number, including the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistics and the burst-significance flags, which is
a larger disturbance than the error. The correct wording throughout is
that the simulation is \textbf{approximately} calibrated to each
subject's measured error rate; no claim of exact reproduction is made.

\subsection{Confidence is conditioned on correctness, but not on class}

The repair of Section~\ref{sec:repair} splits each subject's measured
posteriors into correct and incorrect pools. It does \emph{not} further
condition on which class was cued, although confidence varies strongly by
class. The reason is sparsity: $3$ of the $36$ subject $\times$ class
cells contain $6$ or fewer correct trials, the sparsest being subject 6's
\texttt{right\_hand} with $2$ correct against $70$ wrong. Class-conditional
sampling would need a declared shrinkage rule and would still be
unreliable in those cells. The simplification is stated here rather than
left implicit.

\subsection{A latent ordering hazard in the command channel}

When several decisions arrive in the same simulation step the channel
promotes the newest of them, but does not compare that against the
decision already active. An older command arriving late could therefore
in principle replace a newer one. On this dataset it cannot: latency lies
in $[2.0, 3.0]$\,s (the floor is the end of the $2$\,s decoder window;
the ceiling is the \texttt{max\_s} clip) against a $2.0$\,s decision
interval, so the
maximum spread is $1.0$\,s and overtaking would require more than
$2.0$\,s. In fact it cannot occur at all: a decision issued at $t$ arrives
no later than $t + 3$, while the next, issued at $t + 2$, arrives no
earlier than $t + 4$, so arrival order is strictly issue order. It is
recorded because it would become reachable if the decision interval were
shortened or the latency ceiling raised.

\subsection{One deliverable is unverified}

The symbolic derivation in \texttt{mathematica/stability.wl} was
\textbf{never executed}: the development machine has the
\texttt{wolframscript} launcher on \texttt{PATH} but no Wolfram kernel
installed. The file ships with a prominent NOT VERIFIED banner and with
every expected numeric result quoted inline in its comments, so a
disagreement on first run is immediately visible rather than silent.
Everything it derives --- the magnitude balance, the quadratic, $\astar$,
the exactness criterion, the Pad\'e comparison --- is independently
verified in Python and in base MATLAB. We flag it here rather than let a
reader discover it.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{What was established}

A brain--computer interface decoding motor imagery from EEG is, on real
cross-session data, wrong $56\%$ of the time --- and a wheelchair driven
directly from it collides in $88\%$ of episodes. Splitting control
authority with an autonomous navigator recovers the task. A fixed split
works only if the user's share is very small ($\alpha \le 0.1$ gives
$1.000$; $\alpha = 0.5$ gives $0.161$); conditioning the split on
situational risk achieves the same success while letting authority rise
when the path is clear. Near an obstacle the average of two safe
commands can be unsafe, because the safe set of a nonholonomic vehicle
is not convex, and we demonstrate exactly that in closed loop. Part of
the failure of high-authority policies also comes from the simulated
operator, which does not avoid obstacles.

Gating authority on situational risk works. It reaches $99.6\%$ success
with a $0.4\%$ collision rate, and it holds $0.98$--$1.00$ for every
subject including one whose decoder is below chance --- breaking the
link between decoder quality and task success that fixed blending and
the safety filter exhibit.

On the theoretical side, the transport-delay boundary of the blended
loop admits an exact solution. The delay cancels identically from the
magnitude condition, reducing an infinite-dimensional stability question
to a quadratic in $\omega^2$, and yielding both $\tau_{\max}(\alpha)$
and a closed-form criterion, $b(\astar) \ge 0$, for when the
delay-independent authority bound is tight. The result is checked four
ways, including an independent implementation in another language that
shares no code with ours. It is exact for a linear double-integrator
model of the blended loop; it does not transfer to the simulated
wheelchair, where the channel delay enters the reference rather than
the heading feedback (Section~\ref{sec:delaytruth}).

And three findings run against the project's own thesis: the adaptive
policy is not significantly better than pure autonomy; the risk term
rather than the confidence term is what keeps it safe, which required
renaming the mechanism; and the errors this dataset produces are not
bursty, which required converting an assumption into a swept variable.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

In descending order of value:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A user study, or at minimum an agency metric.} The central
argument for adaptive over autonomous control is that it returns
authority. Nothing in this work shows that matters to a user. Until it
does, \vauto{} remains at least as good as \vadapt{} on every
task-performance metric.

\item \textbf{Switched-system or LPV stability for time-varying
$\alpha$.} A dwell-time condition on the Schmitt trigger, or a common
Lyapunov function across the $\alpha$ range, would convert the
frozen-$\alpha$ guide into a proof for the linear model; it would still
not cover the simulated wheelchair.

\item \textbf{The discrete-command modality.} Running the same six
policies with a genuine four-symbol channel would test whether the
ranking survives the substitution of Section~\ref{sec:modality}. The
architecture already supports it as a configuration switch.

\item \textbf{An authority-allocation metric.} Remark~\ref{rem:frontier}
identifies the measurement this study lacks: authority delivered per unit
of risk incurred, which would separate adaptive allocation from uniform
attenuation on the frontier rather than relying on a single trace.

\item \textbf{Risk without a map.} If risk carries
the safety, then estimating risk from range sensing alone --- no prior map,
no exact localisation --- is the highest-value robustness extension, and
it is what would make the method deployable.
\end{enumerate}

separately, which is what \texttt{pytest -q} reports. The $35$ tests
